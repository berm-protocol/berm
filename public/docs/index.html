The garden around the wall

Why Berm exists, what a berm actually is, and the one thing every previous answer to this problem got wrong.

Every platform makes the same offer, and it is a good one. Bring the people who want to hear from you. We will handle the hosting, the search, the feed, the spam, the abuse reports, the bill. You will never think about any of it again.

Almost everyone takes the offer, because the alternative is a website nobody visits. That is not weakness. It is arithmetic.

The cost is invisible for years, and then it is total. Your account is not where your identity is kept — your account is your identity. The name, the work, the archive, and every person who chose to hear from you exist as rows in somebody else's database, joined to a single primary key. When that key is revoked, none of it degrades. It vanishes.

Not locked out. Gone. There are people who signed up to something a decade ago with an account they have since lost, and there is no path back that anyone can offer them, because there is nothing left to point at. The work did not move somewhere else. It stopped existing.

What everyone gets wrong about the answer#

The usual response is: leave. Own your own site. Build your mailing list. Come to our platform, which is better and will not do that to you.

Nobody leaves. The reason is not laziness or lock-in psychology — it is that the people are inside. Telling someone to abandon the audience they spent years earning is not advice, it is a dare. And the platform that promises never to do that to you is one funding round away from a new policy.

The second response is to ask to be let further in. Build on the API. Become a partner. Get the good rate limits. That works right up until the terms change on a Tuesday, and every dead client of every dead API died exactly that way.

Both answers accept the same premise: that there are two places, inside the wall and nowhere.

What a berm is#

In fortification, a berm is the strip of level ground at the foot of a wall, between the wall itself and the ditch. It is not inside. It is not out in the open field. It is the ground where you can actually stand and build, close enough to the wall to matter and outside it enough to be yours.

That is the third place, and it is the whole idea.

You do not leave X. You do not ask X for permission. You keep posting exactly where you post now, to exactly the people you reach now. What changes is where you are kept.

What actually changes#

Your identity becomes a key you hold. Not an account someone issues you — a key, the way a house key is yours whether or not the locksmith is still in business.

Your X handle becomes a claim attached to that key, published by you and checkable by anyone. It is a label on your identity rather than the thing your identity is made of. If the handle goes, the identity does not go with it.

Your work gets signed and stored on an open network of relays. Anyone can host a copy. Nobody can delete the copies they do not hold, and nobody can quietly change one, because a signature that has been tampered with stops verifying and says so.

X stays what it is good at: the place you are seen. It simply stops being the place you exist. Those were always two different jobs, and only one of them needed to be rented.

What this does not do#

The honest limits, because a story that leaves them out is an advertisement.

It will not get you reach. X decides who sees your links, and posts with links do worse than posts without them. Nothing here changes that, and anyone selling you the opposite is selling you something. What it protects is your audience after a policy change, not your distribution before one.

Writing an X Article still needs X's permission. Reading and identity are yours. Publishing into the garden remains the garden's business.

The easiest way in is not yet the safest. The passkey on-ramp — the one that takes a few seconds and requires no wallet, no extension, no seed phrase — depends on a domain name staying in honest hands. That is a real dependency, we did not notice it for a long time, and it has its own section rather than a footnote. The exits exist and work today; the front door is still being rebuilt.

The part worth remembering#

The wall is not the enemy. Walls are the reason gardens work at all — they keep the noise out and make the place worth visiting. Nobody here is trying to knock one down, and a project that spent its energy on that would deserve to fail.

The problem was never that the garden has a wall. It is that there was nowhere to stand when the gate closed.

Now there is.
